Hardware + Simulation

Pick-and-Place on a 4‑DOF Arm, From Simulation to Hardware

Technical Write-Up · ROS 2 Jazzy · MoveIt 2 · ESP32-S3

  • Built a ROS 2 and MoveIt 2 inverse-kinematics pick-and-place motion planning pipeline for a custom 4-DOF 3D-printed arm, using the arm's URDF model, TF2 frames and RViz simulation.
  • Deployed the pipeline sim-to-real on hardware, deriving per-joint velocity limits from bench speed sweeps and applying Ruckig jerk-limited smoothing for continuous-acceleration (S-curve) motion.

This page goes into more detail than the portfolio has room for. The arm is my own design: 3D printed, with a stepper on every joint and no encoders or limit switches. I wanted one manipulation stack that runs in simulation and on the real arm without changing any code, covering the whole chain from the CAD model to the motors. The task it runs is picking up a 608ZZ ball bearing and putting it down somewhere else.

How It Fits Together

MoveIt plans a trajectory once and hands it to a controller through two ROS 2 actions: FollowJointTrajectory for the arm and GripperCommand for the gripper. I wrote two nodes that serve those actions. arm_fake_driver publishes the joint states so RViz shows the motion. traj_driver sends the trajectory over serial to the ESP32, which runs it on the motors. Both report back on /joint_states.

To MoveIt the two backends look identical, so switching between the simulation and the real arm is a change in the launch file. The planner, collision model, trajectory processing and visualisation are the same code either way.

On the ROS side it uses actions (I wrote both servers and clients), the /joint_states, /display_planned_path and /monitored_planning_scene topics, and TF2 through robot_state_publisher. The whole task is set through parameters. Python launch files cover the simulation, hardware and calibration setups. Executors are multithreaded with reentrant callback groups, and one client sits on its own single-threaded executor to keep it isolated. Everything builds with colcon.

Motion Planning

The Planning Pipeline

I set the pipeline up explicitly instead of relying on MoveIt's defaults. Before planning, the request adapters run ResolveConstraintFrames, ValidateWorkspaceBounds and CheckStartStateBounds. OMPL's RRTConnect then finds a path, with the projection evaluator set on the two joints that do most of the moving. After planning, the response adapters run in this order: AddTimeOptimalParameterization, AddRuckigTrajectorySmoothing, ValidateSolution, DisplayMotionPath.

The order matters. OMPL returns a geometric path with no timing. TOTG adds timing that respects each joint's velocity and acceleration limits, and Ruckig then reshapes it so jerk stays under its limit too, which gives S-curve motion instead of trapezoidal. On a printed arm running open loop this makes a real difference. Sudden jerk shakes the structure and can make a stepper skip, and with no encoders the arm can't tell that it happened.

Limits Measured on the Bench

The velocity, acceleration and jerk limits in joint_limits.yaml come from testing the motors. For velocity, I ran a motor at a steady step rate and kept raising it until it stopped moving smoothly, with no serial link or streaming involved. That ceiling belongs to the motor, so I converted it through each joint's reduction to get a limit in degrees per second at the joint. The jerk limits come from each joint's acceleration limit over a fixed rise time, and they're what Ruckig enforces.

Because the speed ceiling is set by the motor, doubling a joint's reduction halves its safe output speed. Any regear has to be carried through to the firmware constants and the planner limits.

JointMotionReduction
J1Base yaw4:1
J2Shoulder pitch25:1 planetary
J3Elbow pitch25:1 planetary
J4Wrist pitch6:1

Ruckig works on jerk. It doesn't lower the acceleration; it smooths out the steps in acceleration that appear at every waypoint the planner produces. After adding it, over-limit jerk events dropped from about 10 per second to under 1 per second.

Kinematics

I use two IK solvers. MoveIt's KDL solver handles general planning queries. It's set to position_only_ik, because a 4-DOF arm can't reach an arbitrary 6-DOF pose.

For the grasp poses I wrote a closed-form solver. J2, J3 and J4 all pitch about parallel axes, so if the wrist is slaved as J4 = c − (J2 + J3), the three pitch angles cancel and the tool keeps the same orientation in the world apart from base yaw. Once the base yaw is taken out, that leaves a planar two-link IK problem on the wrist position. The constant c sets the tool convention, and one value points the jaws straight down for top-down picks.

I checked the solver against a separate MuJoCo model of the same arm. They agree to 1×10−15 m, which is floating-point precision.

Collision Model and Planning Scene

The collision model comes from the CAD meshes, with an SRDF listing the link pairs that can never collide. The task node publishes planning-scene diffs to add the bearing, colour it, and attach or detach it. MoveIt's collision shapes don't include an annulus, and a solid cylinder would fill in the bearing's bore, so I generated a triangulated annulus mesh for it. Once the bearing is gripped it's attached to the robot, and the planner treats it as part of the arm while it's carried.

Simulation to Hardware

One Plan for Both

MoveIt plans once, and the same trajectory runs in RViz and on the arm. There's no separate hardware script repeating the motion. The hardware backend implements the same action interface that MoveIt's controller manager already talks to.

Streaming or Upload-and-Execute

I built and compared two ways of running trajectories on the hardware.

StreamingUpload and execute
How it worksThe PC sends one waypoint every 50 msThe whole trajectory is sent first, then run on the ESP32's own clock
Keeps timeThe PCThe ESP32 (micros())
Serial during motionYesNo
ResultJitter from the PC's scheduler−1 to −17 ms of drift over 1.9–7.9 s moves, repeatable

I built the second one after measuring the first. 43.8% of streamed frames arrived later than the window the firmware expected, and the cause was the PC operating system's scheduler rather than the firmware. Running the timing on the microcontroller takes the PC out of the loop while the arm is moving.

Calibration Without Sensors

The arm has no encoders or limit switches, and it can't be back-driven. The firmware zeroes its step counters at power-on and assumes the arm is at home. I built a way to teach a new home position around one rule: teaching home must never move the arm. I tested this by checking that the command frame going out is byte-for-byte the same before and after.

Checking the Arm Follows the Plan

To confirm the hardware runs the trajectory the planner actually produced, I halved one joint's velocity limit in the planner config. The physical cycle went from 32.4 s to 48.0 s, and only the moves dominated by that joint got longer.

The Pick-and-Place Task

  1. Publish the planning scene: the table, and the bearing as a coloured annulus mesh.
  2. Open the gripper through the GripperCommand action.
  3. Solve the grasp with the closed-form IK and come in from above through a pre-grasp point.
  4. Go down, close the gripper and attach the bearing to the robot in the planning scene.
  5. Lift, move to the place position, go down, release and detach.
  6. Back off and return to the rest pose.

Everything is a ROS parameter: the pick and place positions, grasp height offset, the object's shape, size and colour, gripper stroke and closing behaviour, and velocity and acceleration scaling. The same node runs against the simulation or the hardware without changes.

Firmware

  • ESP32-S3 firmware in C++ with a non-blocking main loop.
  • A framed serial protocol with acknowledgements and a state machine for begin, waypoint transfer, execute, query and abort. Every command has a defined response.
  • An on-board buffer that holds a full multi-second trajectory in RAM and runs it on the board's own clock.
  • Stepper control that interpolates between waypoints with feed-forward, plus a bounded correction for tracking error.
  • The gripper servo is driven directly from the LEDC PWM peripheral.
  • Axis signs, angle offsets and the home offset are applied in one place, so ROS and the firmware use the same definition of every joint angle.

Problems I Ran Into

  • Choppy motion. I traced it to the PC's scheduler (43.8% of streamed frames were late) and fixed it by running trajectories on the microcontroller.
  • A joint going over its acceleration limit. The hardware driver was interpolating linearly and throwing away MoveIt's velocity and acceleration data, which drove one joint at 2.5× its acceleration limit. I replaced it with C2-continuous quintic Hermite interpolation.
  • Stale frames after a URDF re-export. I compared the centres of mass of parts I hadn't changed. They had all shifted by the same 40 mm, which showed the frames had gone stale rather than the parts moving.
  • Two controllers fighting. A point-to-point trapezoidal profiler was feeding a trajectory follower. It added no safety and the two worked against each other, so I removed the extra layer.

Still to Do

It works, and I'm still developing it. Open items:

  • One SRDF collision pair is marked as never colliding, but the two links come within 0.1 mm when the arm is folded.
  • The microcontroller sometimes needs unplugging and plugging back in. My best guess is a brownout when the servo stalls, but I haven't confirmed it.
  • Only one of the four joints has been properly speed-tested. The others use its limit to be safe.
  • Grip force comes from stalling a position-controlled servo. It works, but it really needs force feedback, which the arm doesn't have yet.
